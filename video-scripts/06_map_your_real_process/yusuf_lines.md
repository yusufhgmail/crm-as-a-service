## Step 1: Pick one real job

Step one. Pick one real job. I mean one actual customer from the last month who's already paid. Then get everyone who touched that job in the room. Whoever took the first call, whoever priced it, whoever did the work, and whoever sent the invoice.

Why a real job? Because if you ask people how your process works, they'll tell you how it should work. If you ask what happened on this one job, they'll tell you the truth.

Take this one from our research. There's a property lender with twenty-seven people. They've been in business for ten years, and they're profitable. And the co-founder says, "Everything technically works, but it's all manual, somewhat disconnected." Honestly, that's most companies from the inside. Nothing's actually broken. It just all runs on people.

I'm going to do this with our demo roofing company. It's a made-up company in a real HubSpot account. The job is a hail repair for seven thousand two hundred dollars, and it's marked Closed Won.

## Step 2: Write every step

Step two. Write down every step, from the first contact until the money's in the bank. One sticky note per step, and start every sticky with a verb, so you know someone actually did something. You've got ten minutes. And let the people who did the work say it, not the manager.

So for our roofer, the lead calls. Someone books an inspection. The inspector goes up and takes photos and measurements. Someone writes the estimate. The insurance adjuster approves it, and the homeowner signs. Then materials get ordered and a crew gets scheduled. The job gets done, the final invoice goes out, and it gets paid. That's eleven steps.

Now look at the CRM board next to it. It ends at Closed Won, and for a roofer, that's step six of eleven. The materials, the crew, the invoice, getting paid, all of that happens after the CRM says you're done.

Here's another one. There's a company that sells equipment, and over the years they've paid to customise several CRMs. But for them, one deal can be a sale, an engineering project, an installation and a removal, all at once. They call that "a little different". But put it on sticky notes, and you see it's actually their whole business.

And one rule. If someone says "and then it just happens", ask who does it.

## Step 3: Mark where each step lives

Step three. Under each sticky, mark where that step really happens. Green if it's in the CRM. Red if it's anywhere else, like a spreadsheet, someone's inbox, a text message, the whiteboard, or someone's head.

So for our roofer, the first call is in the CRM, and so is the signed contract. Everything else is red. That's two green and nine red.

One Pipedrive customer thought they could build their proposals inside the CRM. They couldn't. So they ended up in Microsoft Word, opening an old proposal and doing find and replace. Then the proposal didn't send, they emailed it instead, and the CRM lost track of the deal. That's one red sticker, and now the green ones around it stop working too.

Nearly nineteen hundred buyers in our study said their CRM doesn't fit, and around two hundred wrote about the spreadsheets and the re-typing they do next to it. So take five minutes, and put green or red under every sticky. That's it.

## Step 4: Find the bottleneck

Step four. Find the bottleneck. Start with the re-typing. Every time the same information gets typed twice, like the name, the address or the price, draw a red arrow from the first place to the second.

So on our map, the name and address get typed into the estimate. The prices get typed again into the invoice. The job details get copied onto the crew whiteboard, and the payment gets entered in two places. That's four red arrows.

Now, most of those are just annoying. You lose ten minutes here and ten minutes there. That's not what we're looking for. We're looking for the one step that decides how much work you can win. One small company had an automation behind their website form. It broke, and nobody noticed for six months. They say that cost them tens of thousands of dollars in lost revenue.

So ask one question. Where does work pile up and wait? For our roofer, it's the estimate. It only gets written in the evening when someone has time, so the homeowner waits three days, and some of them just call another roofer. Let's say that costs two jobs a month, at seven thousand two hundred dollars a job. That's more than a hundred and seventy thousand dollars a year, from one step. That's your bottleneck. And the people who do the work already know where it is.

## Step 5: Sort it: setting, tier or fit

Step five. Go through every red sticker and every red arrow, and write one letter on it. S, T or F. That stands for setting, tier or fit.

A setting is something your CRM can already do, but nobody set it up. "Book inspection" doesn't need to live on the whiteboard. In HubSpot you go to Settings, Objects, Deals, Pipelines, and you add a stage. That's an S. Just fix it this week.

A tier is something your CRM can do, but only on a bigger plan. Let's say our roofer wants insurance jobs and cash jobs on separate boards.

That's a second pipeline, and when I recorded this, HubSpot's free plan gave you zero extra pipelines.

One company needed just one more pipeline. The next plan up came with fifteen, and it cost around five thousand dollars more a year. So that's fourteen pipelines they'd never use. That's a T, and you decide it at renewal.

And a fit problem is when no setting and no plan does it your way. That could be your insurance approvals, your crew schedule, or how your estimate turns into your invoice. One CEO spent over sixteen thousand dollars on HubSpot, and worked with HubSpot's own team for more than a year. In the end they said, "We had to ask if the tool fit our workflow. For us, it just didn't." That's an F.

So now count them up. The S's are your to-do list for this week. The T's go on your renewal list. And if your bottleneck has an F on it, no setting and no plan is ever going to fix it.
