## Plan

Five steps, and the minutes add up to thirty. And stay for step five. That's where the map tells you what to actually do about it.

## Step 1: Pick one real job

Step one. Pick one real job. Not your process in general. One actual customer, from the last month, that's already paid. And get the people who touched it in the room: whoever took the first call, whoever priced it, whoever did the work, and whoever sent the invoice.

Why a real job? Ask people how your process works, and they tell you how it should work. Ask what happened with one real job, and they tell you the truth.

This is the co-founder of a property lender. Twenty-seven people, ten years in business, profitable. And he says, "Everything technically works, but it's all manual, somewhat disconnected." Honestly, that's most companies from the inside. Nothing is broken. It just all runs on people.

I'll do it with our demo roofing company. It's a made-up company in a real HubSpot account. The job is a hail repair, seven thousand two hundred dollars, marked Closed Won. So pick yours, book thirty minutes, and get those four people in a room.

## Step 2: Write every step

Step two. Write every step, from the first contact to the money in the bank. One sticky note per step. And every sticky starts with a verb, so someone did something. You've got ten minutes. Go in order, and let the people who did the work say it, not the manager.

For our roofer: the lead calls. Someone books an inspection. The inspector goes up, takes photos and measurements. Someone writes the estimate. The insurance adjuster approves it. The homeowner signs. Materials get ordered. A crew gets scheduled. The job's done, with photos. The final invoice goes out. And it gets paid. Eleven steps.

Now look at the CRM board next to it. It ends at Closed Won. For a roofer, that's step six of eleven. The materials, the crew, the invoice, getting paid, all of that happens after the CRM says you're done.

This company sells equipment. They looked at existing CRMs and paid for customisations on some of them over several years. But one deal for them can be a sale, an engineering project, an installation and a removal, all at once. And they call that "a little different". Put it on sticky notes and you see it's their whole business, not a little difference.

And one rule. If someone says "and then it just happens", ask who does it.

So that's steps one and two: one real job, and every step it took, start to paid.

## Step 3: Mark where each step lives

Step three. Under each sticky, mark where that step really lives. Green if it happens in the CRM. Red if it happens anywhere else: a spreadsheet, someone's inbox, a text message, the whiteboard, paper, or someone's head.

So for our demo roofer, the first call is in the CRM. The signed contract is in the CRM. Everything else is red. Two green, nine red.

This Pipedrive customer, in May, thought they could build proposals inside the CRM. They couldn't. So it's Microsoft Word, open an old proposal, find and replace. Then the proposal didn't send, they sent it by email instead, and the CRM lost track of the deal. So one red sticker, and the green ones around it stop working too.

Remember the nearly nineteen hundred buyers who said their CRM doesn't fit? Well, around two hundred buyers in our study wrote about the spreadsheets and the re-typing they do next to their CRM. So, five minutes. Green or red, under every sticky.

## Step 4: Draw the re-typing

Step four. Draw the re-typing. Every time the same information gets typed a second time, the customer's name, the address, the price, the line items, draw a red arrow from where it was typed first to where it's typed again.

So on our map, the name and address get typed into the estimate. The prices get typed again into the invoice. The job details get copied onto the crew whiteboard. And the payment gets entered in two places. Four red arrows.

This Jobber customer, in June, paid for a full year. Their invoice numbers clashed with QuickBooks, so now every invoice has to start in Jobber. And every payment gets checked in QuickBooks for late fees, then typed in again. That's a red arrow on every payment, for a year.

Then put a number on each arrow. How many times a week does it happen, times how many minutes. If the office types fifteen estimates into invoices a week, and each takes ten minutes, that's two and a half hours, every week, on one arrow. Ask the person who does it. They know the number exactly.

So that's steps three and four: which steps live outside your CRM, and what the re-typing costs you every week.

## Step 5: Sort it: setting, tier or fit

Step five. Go through every red sticker and every red arrow, and write one letter on it. S, T or F. Setting, tier or fit.

A setting is something your CRM can already do, and nobody set it up. "Book inspection" doesn't need to live on the whiteboard. In HubSpot it's Settings, Objects, Deals, Pipelines, add a stage. That's an S. Fix it this week.

A tier is something your CRM can do, on a bigger plan. Say our roofer wants insurance jobs and cash jobs on separate boards.

That's a second pipeline, and HubSpot's free plan gives you zero extra pipelines.

This company, in March, needed one more pipeline. On their plan, the next level up came with fifteen, and cost around five thousand dollars more a year. Fourteen pipelines they'd never use. That's a T. You decide it at renewal, with the numbers.

And a fit problem is one where no setting and no plan does it your way. Your insurance approvals. Your crew schedule. Your estimate turning into your invoice with your rules. This CEO spent over sixteen thousand dollars on HubSpot, and worked with HubSpot's own team for more than a year. Their answer: "We had to ask if the tool fit our workflow. For us, it just didn't." That's an F.

Five minutes, one letter each. When you're done, count them. The S's are this week's to-do list. The T's go on your renewal list. And the F's, with the hours you wrote next to them, are what your CRM really doesn't do for you.
