## Plan

The prompt's in the description, word for word. It has six parts, and I'll run them on an example as we go.

## Part 1: Who you are

Say you run a heat pump installer with twenty-five people. I made this company up. Part one is just the basics about your company. And the first line says do not recommend a specific CRM product, or you'll probably get a shopping list.

## Part 2: How you sell

Part two is how you sell, the way it actually works on a normal Tuesday. Our installer gets leads from the website and a comparison site, and about half the quotes need a second visit.

## Part 3: How you deliver

Part three is everything from the signed deal until you're paid. Most CRMs stop at won, so people leave this out. Here every unit needs a service visit after twelve months, which they often forget.

I wouldn't write this part yourself. Ask whoever does the invoicing, "Can you write down every step from a signed deal until we're paid? I mean the way it really happens, with the spreadsheets and the things you chase by hand."

## Part 4: The rules

I think part four matters most. If you don't tell it to ask, AI just guesses, and it sounds sure of itself. So the rules make it ask me up to ten questions first.

Look at number four. Does the comparison site send the same lead to your competitors? Here it sends every lead to three installers. And number six, why do about half the quotes need a second visit? I'd want to know both. At thirty quotes a month, that's fifteen extra trips, mostly because the first visit didn't check the electrics.

## Part 5: What you want back

Part five is the plan it writes back, and it opens with where the money's leaking. Eighteen of those thirty quotes get signed. At fifteen thousand dollars a job, the other twelve are worth a hundred and eighty thousand dollars a month, and nobody follows up on them. You won't win all of that, but do the sum for your company. Your quotes, minus signed deals, times your average deal.

You get two pipelines, one for selling and one for delivery. I've done this by hand. A recruitment firm I worked with had stopped using its CRM, because a standard setup couldn't link a recruit to each client's hiring project. So I sat with them and proposed two pipelines, and that's what got them back in.

Here the quote can't go out until the electrics and the outdoor spot are recorded, and that should remove most of those fifteen trips.

The automations come ranked. The twelve-month service visit saves the least time, and it still says build it first, because the warranty depends on it. At two hundred dollars a visit, eighteen installs a month is about forty-three thousand dollars a year, and they often forget those visits.

Each integration comes with what breaks. If the email link fails, new leads sit there while the two other installers call first. At my old company, integrations were the major problem, and we often found out from customers.

And you get three numbers for Monday morning, quotes going cold, overdue money and service visits at risk.

## Part 6: The checklist

Part six is a checklist for before you pay anyone. Who owns the data? How would you get it out? Who changes the setup when your business changes? What does it cost at twice your size? And which part won't a standard CRM cover?

On getting your data out, I wouldn't take a yes. Ask them to export one customer, with the notes and the files, before you sign.

For our installer, a standard CRM may not cover two-person crew scheduling, marking jobs done on a phone, or service dates for every installed unit.

## A real company: Springland

Springland is a real one from our research. They sell pet products wholesale, and Gabriel Ficht built their CRM with AI in about twenty part-time hours. It shows the next action and the bottleneck on every account. And he can ask it, which wholesale accounts haven't reordered in ninety days but were trending up before that? For our installer, that might be which units are past twelve months with no visit booked. He says questions like that are nearly impossible inside someone else's CRM. And he says they're staying on top of three to four times more deals. That's his number, and I can't check it. But in my opinion, that's where the value comes from, insights and actions you couldn't get without the full view of the customer.

## When you run it on your company

When you run it on your own company, number one, write at least a page, and put most of it in parts two and three. Three lines gets you a generic plan.

Secondly, ask it what to cut. In my experience, that's the main question with any CRM. Just paste this in. "What can we cut from this plan and still run the business with as little admin as possible?"

And number three, send it to two people on your team. "This is a first draft of how we'd run our CRM. What's missing, and which step would you never actually do?"

## The email

And here's the email. Send it to every vendor before the demo. "Here's our plan. In the demo, please run one real job of ours, from first contact to final payment. Show us the parts a standard CRM may not cover. And tell us in writing what it costs at twice our size."
