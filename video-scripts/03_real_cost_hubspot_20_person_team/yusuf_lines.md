## Plan

Five lines. The first three are on HubSpot's own price page. The fourth never shows up on your HubSpot invoice. And the last one isn't a cost at all. It's the test that tells you if forty thousand dollars a year is a great deal or a terrible one.

## Line 1: The seats

Line one: the seats. A seat is one person's login. Let's price a twenty-person team on Sales Hub Professional, the tier with the sales automation and reporting.

Here's HubSpot's own page when I recorded this, in November 2026. For twenty people, that's about eighteen hundred to two thousand dollars a month, and you commit for a year. Prices change, so treat that as an example. And that's just the first line.

HubSpot doesn't have one kind of seat. There are Core seats, Sales seats, Service seats and Revenue seats. And some everyday jobs need a specific seat.

Another company wrote in September that they already pay around fifteen thousand euros a year. Then they found out that to create a quote, the most basic step in their sales process, their people need an extra seat. They say their bill is going to twenty-five thousand.

And it's true. HubSpot's own quotes help page says it: you must have a Revenue Hub seat to be able to create quotes. Say five of your twenty people send quotes. That's about another four hundred dollars a month.

The check: Settings, Users and Teams, the Seats tab. Write down every seat type you pay for, how many of each, and the price per seat from your invoice.

## Line 2: The hubs

Line two: the hubs. A hub is a separate HubSpot product with its own price: Sales Hub, Marketing Hub, Service Hub. Say your second is Marketing Hub, for email.

Marketing Hub Professional starts at eight hundred dollars a month, and the page also shows eight hundred and ninety. It comes with three seats for your marketing people. Add it to the seats from line one, and our twenty-person team is now at about three thousand to thirty-three hundred dollars a month. At list price, before anything else.

Here's what it looks like when hubs you pay for never get switched on. A founder signed a three-year contract for five hubs, about fourteen thousand dollars a year. Nearly two years in, three of those five had never been switched on. They say the required onboarding never happened. What they actually use is the basic CRM and ticketing. They asked to change the contract. HubSpot refused. Three years.

Every hub you pay for should do a job. If you can't say what it is, why are you paying?

One thing to watch. When I recorded this, new customers in Europe had started buying HubSpot differently: one plan with seats and credits, not separate hubs. So check which one you're on.

The check: Account and Billing, Subscriptions. List every hub you pay for, and next to each one, the one job it does for your business this month.

So that's lines one and two. Already around three thousand dollars a month for twenty people, and we haven't set anything up yet.

## Line 3: The setup

Line three: the setup. This one is on the price page too, but in the small print.

Both Professional hubs come with a required, one-time onboarding fee. Fifteen hundred dollars for Sales Hub. Three thousand for Marketing Hub. Forty-five hundred dollars before your team logs in.

So year one, at list prices: between forty-one and forty-four thousand dollars. And that's before your quote templates, pipelines and accounting link are built. For that, companies hire a HubSpot partner.

Here's another example, from July. One company says they spent nearly twenty thousand dollars, and four months later still had nothing done. They also say HubSpot's agreement puts the integration between you and a third party, and they quote it: "we are not responsible for our recommended HubSpot expert."

If this doesn't work, who's responsible? Get the answer in writing before you pay anyone.

The check: add up everything you've paid to set HubSpot up: onboarding, partners, migration, training. Then list what's still not finished. About to sign? Get the full setup price, partner included, before you commit to a year.

## Line 4: The glue

Line four: the glue. It never shows up on your HubSpot invoice. It's every app you pay for so HubSpot can talk to the rest of your business: accounting, scheduling, forms, your job system. Zapier, Make, connectors, add-ons.

We found a customer who was already paying for HubSpot Professional and Marketing. They wanted one simple thing: someone fills in a form, and an email goes out. To do it, they needed three separate subscriptions, up to a thousand dollars a month. For a form and an email.

In our study, around two hundred HubSpot customers complained about connecting it to their other tools.

The check: Settings, Integrations, Connected Apps. That's everything plugged into HubSpot. Next to each one, write what you pay every month, and what breaks if it stops.

So that's lines three and four. Now you've got the whole bill. Line five is the one that matters.

## Line 5: The worth-it test

Line five. Is it worth it? Not a question about price. Forty thousand a year can be a great deal, or a terrible one. It depends on one thing: does it do the jobs your business needs, the way your business does them?

Remember the company from the start? That's exactly the test they ran. They need four jobs done, and none of the things their tier was built for. They're paying for the way HubSpot thinks a company sells.

And here's the other side. We found a manufacturer that makes cups. Their sales run on pricing calculators, quotes and contracts, tied to their production planner and stock. So they replaced HubSpot, and several other tools, with software they own. Asked in October 2026 what he saved on HubSpot, the cofounder said roughly ten thousand dollars a month.

In our study, nearly four hundred HubSpot customers complained that it doesn't fit how they work. That's more than consultants and connecting tools put together.

The check: write down every job your CRM must do. Not features, jobs. Like "send a quote from our price list." Or "turn a won deal into a job, and the job into an invoice." Next to each one: does HubSpot do this the way we work? Yes or no. Every no means a spreadsheet somewhere, or someone re-typing numbers, or one more app from line four. Then put your whole bill, lines one to four, next to that list. Now you can see what you're really paying for.

## Is a smaller bill enough?

Lines one and two, you can trim. Drop a hub nobody uses. Get the seat types right. That's real money. But the bill is still built around how HubSpot sells, and that's not how you sell. You hire people, the seats grow. And if line five gave you a long list of no's, trimming doesn't fix that.
