## Lesson 1: A cheaper one can have the same staircase

Number one, a cheaper CRM can have the same staircase. You compare entry plans, and then you end up paying for the plan that does what you need.

Take this one, from June. One business moved from Workiz to Jobber, mainly for better pricing. They were told Core would show item costs on quotes, and it didn't. So they upgraded to Connect and hooked up DocuSign, and only names and emails came over. So they upgraded again, to Grow, for a quoting feature from Jobber's own video, and they were told it isn't available anymore. They say every time, the only answer they get is to upgrade.

Here's Jobber's pricing page when I recorded this. Prices move, so take it as an example. For one user, Core is twenty-nine dollars a month and Grow is a hundred and forty-nine. That's about fourteen hundred dollars more a year for one person, and they still didn't get the feature.

So write down the five things your team can't work without. Find the cheapest plan that does all five, and multiply it by the people you'll have in two years. Say that's a hundred dollars a seat and fifteen people, so eighteen thousand dollars a year before add-ons. Then make the rep show you all five working on that exact plan, and ask by email what fixing it costs if one doesn't.

## Lesson 2: The new one doesn't fit either

The second one's from G2. A team at a mid-size company picked Freshsales, and they say the only upside is that it's cheaper than HubSpot. They paid for a full year, stopped using it, and say Excel is much better for them. So the new one didn't fit either.

At my old company, out of every hundred customers, maybe three were happy with the standard setup. When you buy a CRM, what you're actually buying is a standard process that you have to adapt to. So if you switch, you've just swapped one company's process for another's.

A demo won't show you that. So take one real job from last month, and write down every step from the first call to the invoice, and who did it. There's a whole video on that, linked below. Then run your three most common jobs in the trial, with the people who'll use it. If they can't do their real week in there, don't sign.

## Lesson 3: Nobody helps after you sign

Three is what happens after you sign. A manager at an IT company with eleven to fifty people wrote on Capterra that their CFO moved them from Salesforce to Zoho. When they saw the setup, they couldn't get the changes done, and they say the people who set it up weren't cooperative.

So during the trial, open two real support tickets about your own setup, and time the answers. If you just get a link to a help article, count that as a fail. Then email the rep and ask, after we go live, who makes changes for us, how fast, and what does it cost per hour?

## Lesson 4: The move breaks what worked

Four is the one I promised you, because I've been on the wrong side of it. In 2020, one of our customers was leaving us for another CRM, and they couldn't export their notes or tasks. And I had to write to them, unfortunately there's nothing more I can see that we can do. One of their users said moving it by hand could take three months. Their sales director wrote, that's just unbelievable, with two exclamation marks. In the end the notes on contacts came out, for two hundred and fifty dollars. The other notes and all the tasks stayed behind.

Names and emails usually come over fine. It's the history, the custom fields, the email sending and the connected tools that break.

So before you sign the new one, ask in writing what you can take with you if you leave. And test the move before you cancel the old one. Number one, export a hundred real records with their history, put them in the trial, and check them field by field. Secondly, send more than a hundred real emails from a spare address, because that's about where the company from the start got blocked. Number three, connect your accounting tool. And keep the old system until all three work. Yes, that means paying for both for a month or so. Just do it anyway.

## Lesson 5: Upgrades only go one way

And five, that CEO. They run a financial services company with eleven to fifty people. They started on HubSpot's entry plan, and they upgraded as they grew. Then their costs shot up and their power dialer didn't work, and they left one month after signing. They still have to pay out the other eleven months, and they say that's smarter than losing more money working around the limits.

I think that's right, if you're stuck now. Say you've got eight months left at two thousand dollars a month. That's sixteen thousand, and you pay it if you stay and you pay it if you go. So just work out what staying costs you on top of that. Take the hours your team spends on workarounds each month, times what you pay them, plus any deals you lost because of it.

And upgrades only go one way. HubSpot's terms actually put that in writing. You can't downgrade during your current term. Your plan renews automatically for up to a year. And at renewal the price can go up to full list price, with thirty days' notice.

So before you sign, and before every upgrade, get two answers in writing. When can we go back down? And what's the last day we can cancel? Ask for the shortest first term they'll give you, and put that date in your calendar the day you sign.

## When another switch won't fix it

The fit, the help, the move and the contract just follow you to the next CRM. And sometimes the thing no standard CRM does is what's holding the business back.

Take Springland, a pet products company. Their demand comes from a dozen places, like Amazon, Faire and wholesale. A guy there called Gabriel Ficht built their own CRM with AI, in about twenty hours over two weeks. It pulls every inbox into one view per customer, and it pulls together the wholesale orders from every platform they sell on. Then it shows the next action and the current bottleneck on every account. He says sales ops time is down more than eighty percent, and they're staying on top of three to four times more deals. That's his number, and one company isn't proof. But I don't think you get that from switching to a new logo.

Or Partition Pros, a construction business. They say they go from napkin drawings to order placement in under fifteen minutes, on their own CRM, 3D designer and customer portal. They don't say what that did for sales, but I'd bet it wins them jobs. The guy behind it is a software engineer, which most owners aren't. If the way you sell is what sets you apart, that's when I'd own the CRM.
