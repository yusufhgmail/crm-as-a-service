Title: Video script rules

[All video scripts](README.md) · [How a script should sound](SOUNDS_HUMAN.md) · [Process and scoring](CLAUDE.md)

Yusuf's rules for every video, whoever writes it. This file covers what a script says and how it's built, from the first line to the last. [SOUNDS_HUMAN.md](SOUNDS_HUMAN.md) covers how the words sound. Read this file, then that one, before you write or change a script.

**What the videos are for:** attract the right people, give them real value, and frame their problems so they view our offer favorably and wonder what we do and how we can help. Every rule here and in David's template serves that goal. Nothing has to appear in every video, including checks, recaps and steps. Keep a check when it helps the viewer or frames the problem. Cut it when it's only there to fill a slot.

- Not like this: "The check: write down each step a customer goes through in your business." after every reason, because the template has one.
- But like this: "That's what a CRM built for everyone does. Your business bends around it, and the parts that don't bend end up in spreadsheets and in people's heads."

**Who's watching:** a sales manager or CEO of a company of at least ten people paying real money for its CRM. They're busy, they aren't technical, and they hear each line once, maybe half listening.

# Before you write

**Say something new.** Check that the video gives viewers something the earlier videos haven't. Video 4 was dropped because it mostly repeated videos 1 to 3.

**Check it against the study.** Every video rests on one study, kept in David's private repo. Check every number and every "a lot of people" against it before it goes in a script. Use the figure that matches the video: all CRM buyers, HubSpot customers or Salesforce customers. Check the size of a group before you call it big, because a phrase like "thinking about leaving" can sound like a huge group when only a few customers in the study say it.

**Write the argument first.** Put the video's argument in a few plain sentences, and give every section one job. If a section doesn't serve the argument, cut it, however true it is.

**Find a core point that leads to owning.** Every video teaches something the viewer can use, but its core point has to be bigger than a tip. Start from the video's topic and ask what the viewer has had to live with, what's different now, and what this would look like in one system they own. Keep the practical steps and put that option beside them.

- Not like this: "So I'd keep HubSpot and replace just the one step that's losing you the most money."
- But like this: "So you're not stuck any more, because you don't have to move everything at once."

**Every line earns its place.** People want information, not filler. Each line must give the viewer something or do a job for us. Say everything in the fewest words that keep the hook, the excitement and the information.

- Not like this: "So here's your homework." or "If you watch this and change nothing, nothing changes."
- Not like this: "We didn't send out a survey. We read public posts and reviews." How we ran the study belongs in the description.
- But like this: "So book thirty minutes with your team this week."

# The opening

**Say what the video is about first.** The opening lines must tell the viewer what this video covers and why it's worth watching. Don't open with the conclusion, because it means little before the viewer knows the topic.

- Not like this: "If one step in your sales is losing you money, you probably don't have to leave HubSpot to fix it."
- But like this: "Many of you have been talking to us about replacing your entire CRM in one big project. I'd strongly recommend against that. Here's how I'd do it instead, and why."

**Say who it's for by naming their problem.** Name a problem viewers recognize, using what the research found where we can, then say what the video does about it.

- Not like this: "If you're on HubSpot, or you're about to sign, and you've never seen the whole bill in one place, this one's for you."
- But like this, with a group and a number you've checked in the study for this video: "In our research, [number] HubSpot customers said the bill kept climbing after they signed. If that's you, this video shows you where the money goes, so you can tell if your CRM is the problem."

**Promise what they'll get, one thing to a sentence.** Name what the viewer can do by the end. A list of topics is easy to ignore, and it's hard for Yusuf to say.

- Not like this: "You'll learn how to X, how to Y and how to Z."
- But like this: "By the end you'll have admin access back."

**Don't open on a made-up scene.** Name the problem in the first sentence, in words the viewer already uses. A staged example sounds contrived, and it makes them wait to find out what the video is about.

- Not like this: "Say a customer calls you and asks, 'Why did I get a reminder? I paid two weeks ago.' You look it up."
- But like this: "The major problem on any CRM is getting it to talk to your other tools."

**Keep the hook, change the words.** We're making 20 videos, so viewers would hear a repeated sentence again and again. Every video still needs a reason to watch to the end. Promise something specific from the end of that video: a question it answers, a callback to the opening story, or a surprise you hold back. Check that the video delivers it, and search the other scripts so the wording and the shape are new. If a number opens the video, the video has to do something with it.

- Not like this: "And stay for step five."
- But like this: "And the last one is the test that tells you if forty thousand dollars a year is a great deal or a terrible one."

# The teaching

**Advise, don't instruct.** Yusuf stops at manual steps and gives his reasoning, so write it that way. Say why people do what they do, from what each of them wants. Take the boss's side first, then say honestly what you think, with "I think" or "in my experience" where it's really his view. Answer "that's no big deal" before the viewer thinks it, like "It may feel like it's just one field, but imagine every single field." Do this where it matters, not on every step, and keep "I've seen" to what a source backs.

- Not like this: "The CRM costs the salespeople time and gives them very little back, so pushing them harder won't fix it."
- But like this: "The CRM costs the salespeople time and gives them very little back, so pushing them harder won't fix it. You're giving them conflicting incentives. You ask them to do admin they think is unnecessary and to reach their quota. So they're obviously going to chase the quota, not the admin."
- Not like this: "So ask your team what they call each step, and change the stages to match."
- But like this: "There can be good reasons for those stages. But if your team doesn't follow them anyway, I'm going to bet that you're wrong and that your salespeople are actually correct. So ask your team what they call each step, and change the stages to match."
- Not like this: "If they don't use a field and nobody else does anything with it, make it optional."
- But like this: "If these fields don't help them close deals, do you really need them? I understand, those fields can be very valuable for data. But forcing salespeople to fill them in is really not what they were hired for."

**Make every step something they can do.** A step should come out of what was just said, so the viewer knows why they're doing it. Give one action in one sentence, because Yusuf skips long checks when he reads from the prompter. Then say how to decide what the result means.

- Not like this: "Count the systems yours would have to connect to."
- But like this: "Count the number of systems your CRM would have to connect to, because if it's just one or two, you can probably do it."

**Name real tools.** When a script talks about a kind of tool, name two popular tools that small companies use, so the viewer can picture theirs.

- Not like this: "the tool you send quotes with"
- But like this: "the tool you send quotes with, like PandaDoc or Proposify"

# Facts, stories and proof

**Announce every example.** A listener can't see what "this company" points at. Say it's an example or that we found it in our research, then say "one company", not "this company". If an example is made up, say "say" aloud and show an Example tag on screen.

- Not like this: "This company missed a renewal quote."
- But like this: "Here's an example from our research. One company missed a renewal quote."
- Not like this: "Two CEOs tried building their own CRM. Greg Berry runs Municibid."
- But like this: "We researched what companies say about building their own CRM, and how easy or difficult it is to keep it running. Here are two real examples, linked in the description. Greg Berry runs Municibid."
- But like this, when it's made up: "Say you run a heat pump company with twenty-five people."

**Use recent examples.** A date from 2016 or 2017 makes the point sound old. Use a recent case, or cut it.

**Show the bottleneck, not the admin.** Pick examples where the problem blocks growth: lost deals, lost revenue, work the company can't take on. An annoying extra step isn't worth a viewer's time. Say what fixing it unlocked, with the real numbers if the source has them. A story about why someone couldn't start builds no belief.

- Not like this: "A customer upgrading happens six to eight hundred times a year. And they work that out in Excel."
- But like this: "In 2018 they had a hundred and twenty people and thirteen million dollars in revenue. Leads were falling through the cracks. They built their own CRM. The next year they had a hundred people and nineteen million."

**Tell it in order, then say what it means.** Tell a story in the order it happened. Give the set-up one fact to a sentence, because long set-ups cost Yusuf retakes. Then say what it means, and make sure that point is what happened in that story.

- Not like this: "They left for another rented CRM. You can switch to a new logo and just hit the same wall." (The new CRM worked for them.)
- But like this: "They moved to another rented CRM that could. And that's fine, when someone already sells what you need. But if your work doesn't fit anybody's screens, you can switch to a new logo and just hit the same wall."

**Proof has to help the viewer.** That comes first. When a script cites Yusuf's own work, use it for what the viewer should do next, not as a boast.

- Not like this: "I predicted all of this back in June 2023."
- But like this: "I wrote about this in June 2023. My point was that the costs don't disappear, they just move. So the big vendors keep setting the price. That means you shouldn't wait for your CRM bill to come down on its own."

**Never undercut your own proof.** If a source is too weak to stand behind, cut it. If it's strong enough, say who said it and move on. Put the name first, because a listener can't see quote marks. "He says" is all the honesty a claim needs, and a limit stays in only when it changes what the viewer should do.

- Not like this: "That's one comment, and I can't check it." or "We never got to test that."
- But like this: "He says they're staying on top of three to four times more deals."

**Let the reasoning lead to owning a CRM.** A pile of cases doesn't lead anywhere. Say why the problem comes from renting, then why building around the company fixes it.

- Yusuf's line: "Whereas if you were to build something from ground up with an experienced company that's done it multiple times, it's going to be much easier to get exactly what you want without all the bloat."

**Keep it true later.** Videos stay online for years, and prices and plans change. Give the total instead of the pricing model when you can. Say once when the prices were checked, call them an example, and put a "Prices as of" tag on price screens. Never say "today", "this month" or "this October". Yusuf says those naturally, so give him "when I recorded this".

- Not like this: "This is HubSpot's own page today. Ninety to a hundred dollars a seat per month."
- But like this: "Here's HubSpot's own page when I recorded this, in November 2026. For twenty people, that's about eighteen hundred to two thousand dollars a month. Prices change, so treat that as an example."

# The end

**Pitch in this order.** Many viewers don't know who we are, so say what we do before saying who doesn't need us. Tie it to this video's problem, say who we are and what they get, say what makes it easy and safe (we build it step by step while their team keeps working in the old CRM, we move the data, they own the code, no long contract), say who it's for, then give one next step: the fit check. Say "at least ten people", never "ten to fifty". Never give a price for CRM From Within.

- Not like this: "If you're a two-person team, you don't need us." before saying what we do.
- But like this: "That's why I started CRM From Within. We build you a CRM around how your company sells and delivers. We move your data over and we maintain it. You own the code, there's no long contract, and if you leave, you take everything. It's for companies of at least ten people that a standard CRM doesn't fit. If a standard setup works for you, keep it."

**Say what makes us different.** Our USP is one system that does a company's entire process, from finding customers to delivering the work, with no integrations, fully adapted to them, and they own it. Wherever a video's problem comes from renting a CRM or from separate tools, show both options: fix it where they are, or have it built into that one system. Then paint the picture of that system, from this video's topic, before the pitch. Each video needs its own picture and its own words.

- Not like this: "We build you a CRM from scratch around how your company works, and if you keep QuickBooks, we connect it the way you need."
- But like this: "We build you a CRM from scratch around how your company works, from the first lead to the last payment, so you don't need all those other tools."

**Keep the fit check and the comment question short.** Give each one short sentence, and word them differently in each video.

**Stop when you're done.** No recap of homework after the pitch, and no closing line that says nothing new.

- Not like this: "And this week, just start with your renewal date and that export."

# Yusuf and his old company

**Never name FunnelBud.** Call it his old CRM SaaS company that he sold, in spoken lines, on-screen tags, descriptions and social posts. It was a SaaS company, not an agency. He says: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it." He sold it in July 2025, so say "in 2025", never "a few years ago". Titles say "Ex-SaaS Founder", never "Ex-CRM Agency Owner". Videos already recorded stay as they are.

**Never name FunnelBud customers.** Tell their stories without names. People in our public research can be named if they gave their own name, but only say it when the name helps the viewer.

**Keep his old company and the new work apart.** His old company is his credibility. The study, the videos and what we sell are his company, CRM From Within. When the two come close together, say which is which. Never say "new company": it reduces credibility. Say "my company" or "CRM From Within".

- Not like this: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it. And we studied what more than five thousand CRM buyers say."
- But like this: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it. Now, at my company, CRM From Within, we've studied what more than five thousand CRM buyers say."
- Not like this: "Me and my team set up CRMs for hundreds of companies. So I can tell you it's really expensive to maintain."
- But like this: "Me and my team set up CRMs for hundreds of companies. Now, I run a company that builds custom CRMs for you. So I can tell you it's really expensive to maintain."

**His old company always looks good.** "I can't make my old company look bad. I don't want to be seen as having sold a crappy product that nobody uses." (Yusuf, 5 Nov 2026) Never say its customers stopped using it, cancelled or complained, that it missed something, that customers found its problems first, or that it ran on another company's software. His old company is credibility: what he built, who it served, what he learned. Make the same point as a truth about every CRM, or as something he learned.

- Not like this: "At my old company, our notes on one customer said their sales team used the CRM. Two years later they cancelled."
- But like this: "A sales team can stop using a CRM, and nobody says anything. ... Me and my team set up CRMs for hundreds of companies, so I've seen this happen, and I've seen how to fix it."
- Not like this: "At my old company, we often found out from customers that a connection had broken."
- But like this: "Setting up CRMs for hundreds of companies taught me that connections are what break most, on any CRM."

**His old company's numbers show what's true of every rented CRM.** Say the general truth first, so it doesn't sound like his product was bad.

- Not like this: "At my old company, out of a hundred customers, maybe three were happy with the standard setup. Everyone else asked for changes."
- But like this: "Nobody's really happy with a standard CRM, because it gives you someone else's process. I saw this at my old company. Out of every hundred customers, maybe three were happy with the standard setup."

**Check old-company claims against the record.** Yusuf can't remember every detail, so read the original note or sheet and say only what it shows. A few extreme customers are not the typical one.

- Not like this: "I've seen a CRM need twenty-six to forty hours of support a month."
- But like this: "At my old company, the typical customer took about three hours of setup and support a month. But a few took twenty-six to forty."

**Never invent his experience.** "I've seen" or "I've experienced this myself" needs a source, and any new opinion goes to him to confirm. For what his old company did, say "me and my team", never "I've set up". Never make up a case, number, quote, story or feeling to make a line sound real.

**An agency and CRM software are two different things.** Leaving an agency means keeping the CRM and changing who looks after it. Leaving CRM software means moving to a different CRM. Say which one you mean, and never call a software company an agency. We target people leaving their CRM system, not people leaving an agency.

- Not like this: "When a company leaves whoever set up its CRM."
- But like this: "When a company keeps its CRM but leaves the agency that set it up."

# After every change

For every change to a script, however small:

1. Check that it fits the theme and the text around it, so the script makes sense in full.
2. Ask if it's a general mistake. If so, fix it in every script not yet recorded, and leave recorded videos alone.
3. Give each of those fixes the same check in its own script.

Then run the Optimized process, and have David or Yusuf read the script before it's recorded. [CLAUDE.md](CLAUDE.md) has both.
