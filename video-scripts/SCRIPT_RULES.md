Title: Video script rules

[All video scripts](README.md)

Yusuf's corrections to the scripts. Read this before writing or changing a script, and follow it in every new video, whoever writes it.

**What the videos are for:** attract the right people, give them real value, and frame their problems so they view our offer favorably and wonder what we do and how we can help. Every rule here and in David's template serves that goal. Nothing has to appear in every video, including checks, recaps and steps. Keep a check when it helps the viewer or frames the problem. Cut it when it's only there to fill a slot.

- Not like this: "The check: write down each step a customer goes through in your business." after every reason, because the template has one.
- But like this: "That's what a CRM built for everyone does. Your business bends around it, and the parts that don't bend end up in spreadsheets and in people's heads."

For every change to a script, however small:

1. Check that it fits the theme and the text around it, so the script makes sense in full.
2. Ask if it's a general mistake. If so, fix it in every script not yet recorded, and leave recorded videos alone.
3. Give each of those fixes the same check in its own script.

# The study

Every video rests on one study, kept in David's private repo. Check every number and every "a lot of people" against it before it goes in a script. Use the figure that matches the video: all CRM buyers, HubSpot customers or Salesforce customers.

# Spoken lines

**Announce every example.** A listener can't see what "this company" points at. Say it's an example or that we found it in our research, then say "one company", not "this company".

- Not like this: "This company missed a renewal quote."
- But like this: "Here's an example from our research. One company missed a renewal quote."

**Open with the problem.** "Who it's for" names a problem viewers recognize, using what the research found where we can, then says what the video does about it.

- Not like this: "If you're on HubSpot, or you're about to sign, and you've never seen the whole bill in one place, this one's for you."
- But like this: "The biggest group, around twenty-five hundred of them, are still on their CRM and thinking about leaving. If that's you, this video shows you why they want out, so you can tell whether your CRM is the problem."

**Say something new.** Before filming, check that a video gives viewers something the earlier videos haven't. Video 4 was dropped because it mostly repeated videos 1 to 3.

**Sound like a person talking, and not like AI.** [SOUNDS_HUMAN.md](SOUNDS_HUMAN.md) lists what gives AI away. Shorter never means telegram style. Use full spoken sentences joined with "so", "and" and "but". No colon reveals, no slogans, and no fragments standing in for sentences. Read every line aloud as Yusuf would say it to a friend, using the voice guide in David's repo.

- Not like this: "In 2018: a hundred and twenty people, thirteen million dollars in revenue. Twenty fewer people, six million more."
- But like this: "In 2018 they had a hundred and twenty people and thirteen million dollars in revenue. That's twenty fewer people and six million dollars more."

**Every sentence needs a point, and it has to follow from the one before.** Yusuf, recording video 11: "every single thing that we say has a point and is coherent and fits with everything else". Keep the joining words, say each step of the reasoning, and state the conclusion. Cut any story, line or section you can't give a reason for.

- Not like this: "Contacts move easily when you leave. Years of notes, files, activities and custom records don't. And if your work doesn't fit anybody's screens, you can switch to a new logo and just hit the same wall."
- But like this: "You can move your contacts, but that doesn't mean you can move everything that's important. There may be years of notes and files you can't move as easily. So it's very important to pick the right CRM before you commit all of that to it."

**Never undercut your own proof.** If a source is too weak to stand behind, cut it. If it's strong enough, say who said it and move on. "He says" is all the honesty a claim needs, and a limit stays in only when it changes what the viewer should do.

- Not like this: "That's one comment, and I can't check it." or "We never got to test that."
- But like this: "He says they're staying on top of three to four times more deals."

**Use recent examples.** A date from 2016 or 2017 makes the point sound old. Use a recent case, or cut it.

**No clever phrasing.** Say it the way you'd say it to a friend.

- Not like this: "Year two is nobody's job."
- But like this: "Year two can be a disaster."

**Stop when you're done.** No recap of homework after the pitch, and no closing line that says nothing new.

- Not like this: "And this week, just start with your renewal date and that export."

**Say it so nobody has to think twice.** Use simple, direct sentences, and never point at something the viewer hasn't heard yet. If they'd have to stop and work out what you mean, rewrite it.

- Not like this: "So if you walk in without your own list, you end up using the seller's."
- But like this: "So before you talk to a seller, write down what matters most to your company. If you don't, you'll end up judging the CRM by what the seller wants to show you."

**Don't lean on labels.** Never assume the viewer remembers a word you set up earlier, like "moment", "route" or "part". Each new section starts by saying what it's about in full, as if someone had just started listening.

- Not like this: "The first moment is when you want your CRM to work a little differently."
- But like this: "Renting a CRM starts to hurt when you want it to work a little differently."

**Advise, don't instruct.** Yusuf stops at manual steps and gives his reasoning, so write it that way. Say why people do what they do, take the boss's side first, then say honestly what you think. Answer "that's no big deal" before the viewer thinks it. Do this where it matters, not on every step, and keep "I've seen" to what a source backs.

- Not like this: "The CRM costs the salespeople time and gives them very little back, so pushing them harder won't fix it."
- But like this: "The CRM costs the salespeople time and gives them very little back, so pushing them harder won't fix it. You're giving them conflicting incentives. You ask them to do admin they think is unnecessary and to reach their quota. So they're obviously going to chase the quota, not the admin."
- Not like this: "So ask your team what they call each step, and change the stages to match."
- But like this: "There can be good reasons for those stages. But if your team doesn't follow them anyway, I'm going to bet that you're wrong and that your salespeople are actually correct. So ask your team what they call each step, and change the stages to match."

**Lines must stand alone.** A closing line that points back trips him, and it trips the viewer too.

- Not like this: "And if your team's using the CRM after those fixes, you don't need us."
- But like this: "If your team is already using the CRM that you have today, then you don't need us."

**Run the Optimized process.** Every script gets it before anyone records it, and any edit to the spoken lines means it has to run again. It scores how human the script sounds and how easy it is to understand (those two come first), how well it holds our buyer, whether the viewer can act on it without us, whether it makes them believe a custom CRM can lift the business, and whether it's as short as it can be while making the same points. `optimize.py` tracks this, and [CLAUDE.md](CLAUDE.md) has the process. When a new script comes in without it, tell Yusuf or David forcefully and ask them to approve running it.

**A human reads it before Yusuf records it.** David or Yusuf reads every script first, so obviously bad lines get fixed before filming. The last column of the recordings list shows who read it. Any edit to the spoken lines clears that, so run the Optimized process first and read last.

**Proof has to help the viewer.** That comes first. When a script cites Yusuf's own work, use it for what the viewer should do next, not as a boast.

- Not like this: "I predicted all of this back in June 2023."
- But like this: "I wrote about this in June 2023. My point was that the costs don't disappear, they just move. So the big vendors keep setting the price. That means you shouldn't wait for your CRM bill to come down on its own."

**Check old-company claims against the record.** Yusuf can't remember every detail, so read the original note or sheet and say only what it shows. A few extreme customers are not the typical one.

- Not like this: "I've seen a CRM need twenty-six to forty hours of support a month."
- But like this: "At my old company, the typical customer took about three hours of setup and support a month. But a few took twenty-six to forty."

The point after a story has to be what happened in that story.

- Not like this: "They left for another rented CRM. You can switch to a new logo and just hit the same wall." (The new CRM worked for them.)
- But like this: "They moved to another rented CRM that could. And that's fine, when someone already sells what you need. But if your work doesn't fit anybody's screens, you can switch to a new logo and just hit the same wall."

**Show the bottleneck, not the admin.** Pick examples where the problem blocks growth: lost deals, lost revenue, work the company can't take on. An annoying extra step isn't worth a viewer's time. Say what fixing it unlocked, with the real numbers if the source has them.

- Not like this: "A customer upgrading happens six to eight hundred times a year. And they work that out in Excel."
- But like this: "In 2018 they had a hundred and twenty people and thirteen million dollars in revenue. Leads were falling through the cracks. They built their own CRM. The next year they had a hundred people and nineteen million."

**Keep the hook, change the words.** We're making 20 videos, so viewers would hear a repeated sentence again and again. Every video still needs a reason to watch to the end. Promise something specific from the end of that video: a question it answers, a callback to the opening story, or a surprise you hold back. Check that the video delivers it, and search the other scripts so the wording and the shape are new.

- Not like this: "And stay for step five."
- But like this: "And the last one isn't a cost at all. It's the test that tells you if forty thousand dollars a year is a great deal or a terrible one."

**Every line earns its place.** People want information, not filler. Each line must give the viewer something or do a job for us. Keep the fit check and the comment question to one short sentence, and word them differently in each video. Then say everything in the fewest words that keep the hook, the excitement and the information.

- Not like this: "So here's your homework." or "If you watch this and change nothing, nothing changes." Don't explain how we ran the study either: "We didn't send out a survey. We read public posts and reviews." That belongs in the description.
- But like this: "Your move: book thirty minutes with your team this week."

**Read each new line after the one before it.** Say them aloud in order. A line can't refer back to something the viewer hasn't heard yet.

- Not like this: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it. And we didn't stop at HubSpot."
- But like this: "My last videos were about HubSpot. For this one, we went wider."

**Pitch in this order.** Many viewers don't know who we are, so say what we do before saying who doesn't need us. Tie it to this video's problem, say who we are and what they get, say what makes it easy and safe (we move the data, they own the code, no long contract), say who it's for, then give one next step: the fit check.

- Not like this: "If you're a two-person team, you don't need us." before saying what we do.
- But like this: "That's why I started CRM From Within. We build you a CRM around how your company sells and delivers. We move your data over and we maintain it. You own the code, there's no long contract, and if you leave, you take everything. It's for companies of at least ten people that a standard CRM doesn't fit. If a standard setup works for you, keep it."

**Keep it true later.** Videos stay online for years, and prices and plans change. Give the total instead of the pricing model when you can. Say once when the prices were checked, call them an example, and put a "Prices as of" tag on price screens. Never say "today", "this month" or "this October".

- Not like this: "This is HubSpot's own page today. Ninety to a hundred dollars a seat per month."
- But like this: "Here's HubSpot's own page when I recorded this, in November 2026. For twenty people, that's about eighteen hundred to two thousand dollars a month. Prices change, so treat that as an example."

# Facts about Yusuf

**Never name FunnelBud.** Call it his old CRM SaaS company that he sold, in spoken lines, on-screen tags, descriptions and social posts. It was a SaaS company, not an agency. He says: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it." He sold it in July 2025, so say "in 2025", never "a few years ago". Titles say "Ex-SaaS Founder", never "Ex-CRM Agency Owner". Videos already recorded stay as they are.

**Keep his old company and the new work apart.** His old company is his credibility. The study, the videos and what we sell are his company, CRM From Within. When the two come close together, say which is which. Never say "new company": it reduces credibility. Say "my company" or "CRM From Within".

- Not like this: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it. And we studied what more than five thousand CRM buyers say."
- But like this: "I built a CRM SaaS company, and I sold it. Now, at my company, CRM From Within, we've studied what more than five thousand CRM buyers say."
**His old company always looks good.** "I can't make my old company look bad. I don't want to be seen as having sold a crappy product that nobody uses." (Yusuf, 5 Nov 2026) Never say its customers stopped using it, cancelled or complained, that it missed something, that customers found its problems first, or that it ran on another company's software. His old company is credibility: what he built, who it served, what he learned. Make the same point as a truth about every CRM, or as something he learned.

- Not like this: "At my old company, our notes on one customer said their sales team used the CRM. Two years later they cancelled."
- But like this: "A sales team can stop using a CRM, and nobody says anything. ... Me and my team set up CRMs for hundreds of companies, so I've seen this happen, and I've seen how to fix it."
- Not like this: "At my old company, we often found out from customers that a connection had broken."
- But like this: "Setting up CRMs for hundreds of companies taught me that connections are what break most, on any CRM."

**His old company's numbers show what's true of every rented CRM.** Say the general truth first, so it doesn't sound like his product was bad.

- Not like this: "At my old company, out of a hundred customers, maybe three were happy with the standard setup. Everyone else asked for changes."
- But like this: "Nobody's really happy with a standard CRM, because it gives you someone else's process. I saw this at my old company. Out of every hundred customers, maybe three were happy with the standard setup."

**Never name FunnelBud customers.** Tell their stories without names. People in our public research can be named if they gave their own name.

**An agency and CRM software are two different things.** Leaving an agency means keeping the CRM and changing who looks after it. Leaving CRM software means moving to a different CRM. Say which one you mean, and never call a software company an agency. We target people leaving their CRM system, not people leaving an agency.

- Not like this: "When a company leaves whoever set up its CRM."
- But like this: "When a company keeps its CRM but leaves the agency that set it up."
