## Step 1: Find your dates, and who gets the emails

Step one, find your dates, and find out who Salesforce is emailing.

The main contract says you renew automatically for another year, unless your order form says otherwise. To stop it, you give written notice at least thirty days before the end, and email counts.

But the help page wants your changes in before ninety days, and then it says anything in the last thirty may be too late. I don't really see how those fit together, so I'd just go early. If your contract ends September thirtieth, put a reminder on June twenty-second. That's a hundred days out, and it's the day you send the email in step four.

The same page says renewal notices go to the billing contact on your contract. If that's someone who left last year, nobody sees the email.

The path to your contracts page is on screen. For every contract, write down the end date, if it's on auto-renew, and who the billing contact is. If it's the wrong person, fix it today.

## Step 2: Read your order form

Step two, read your order form. That's the paper you actually signed, with your prices on it. Almost every rule I just showed you starts with the same words, except as otherwise specified in an order form. And if the two disagree, the order form wins. What I just told you might not even apply to you.

Go through it with a pen. Number one, the term. Salesforce's standard is twelve months, but one rep told a customer they might do better on an eighty-five month term. That customer wrote, my guy, I don't even know if software will exist then.

Secondly, check if it auto-renews, and find your price per licence and the price at renewal. Number three, find when any discount ends, because then you can be back at list price. And number four, go through every product line. Another customer saved thirty thousand dollars by taking out a support plan called Premier Success, and their rep kept putting it back in the DocuSign, six times.

If you can't find them all, ask your account rep for every signed order form, add-ons included. The email's on screen. And if there's a line you didn't ask for, ask why, in writing.

## Step 3: Count your licences

Step three, count your licences. You can add them any day at the same price, but you can only go down at renewal. And if you renew with fewer, they re-price you.

Take this one, from July. A company had shrunk from thirty-something people to twenty-something, and their sales rep was already threatening to cut the discount. That January customer says they halved their licences, and everything went to list price.

Do the sum before you cut. Say you pay for twenty-five licences at two hundred dollars a month, and only eighteen people log in. Those seven cost you about seventeen thousand dollars a year. If your discount goes, that saving shrinks, so you'll want two quotes, one at today's number and one at yours.

Your licence page shows how many you pay for and how many are used. The path's on screen. Then ask each manager who needs a full licence next year, and who hasn't logged in for a month, and decide your number before that reminder goes off.

## Step 4: Send notice in writing, and get a second quote

Step four, send your notice in writing, and get a second quote.

That company with twenty-something people got told by their sales rep that they didn't need to give notice. They gave it anyway, on the last possible day. Then the renewals manager said, as you didn't terminate, we can't do much for you. And they wrote that it was so great to be able to tell him they had.

HubSpot offered them all its hubs for about the price of Salesforce alone. So they gave Salesforce a competitor's quote, and said they were running both CRMs to see which won. They didn't plan to leave at all, and they say the price came down so much it was ridiculous.

Once auto-renew is off, you have to sign a new quote before the end date, or your Salesforce just stops.

At a hundred days, send one email. Just say, this is our written notice under section eleven point two, please do not auto-renew, and confirm it's off. Then ask for two line-by-line quotes, one for today's licences and one for your number, and ask them to confirm next year's price in writing. If the money's big, I'd post it too, to the address in the contract.

Then check your account page says it's off, and get one real quote from another CRM, even if you don't plan to move. Keep every reply, because a phone call is hard to prove later.

## Step 5: Ask the fit question

Step five is the one I care about most. Before you sign for another year, ask if Salesforce actually fits how you work.

At my old company, out of a hundred companies, maybe three were happy with the standard setup. When you buy a CRM, what you're actually buying is a standard process that you have to adapt to. And Salesforce is more like a tool set, so you have to have developers build custom stuff in it. At a company I worked for before that, we had to build our own quoting system on one of these platforms. In my experience, people underestimate that work by something like five times.

Use the thirty-minute map from video six. Take one real job, write every step from first contact to getting paid, and mark everything that happens outside Salesforce. If that list is long, I don't think a better price does much for you. And if the way you sell is what sets you apart, that's when I'd want my own system.

In October, one customer on LinkedIn got a nine percent increase and couldn't remember the last useful new feature. They signed off, BRB while I check if AI tech is ready for us to develop our own CRM in-house. Someone else put it shorter in August. I pay a hundred thousand dollars in Salesforce, replacing it with a custom app, and not renewing my contract in December.

That pet products company is Springland. Gabriel Ficht built their CRM himself, in about twenty hours, part-time, over two weeks. For every account, it shows the next action and the current bottleneck. He says sales ops time is down more than eighty percent, and they're on top of three to four times more deals. Those are his numbers, and I can't check them. But when the system tells you the next action on every account, I can see how one person carries more deals. And he says it gets a little sharper every week.

The story I tell most is about a solar startup in Sweden, with about fifteen sellers. All they used in HubSpot was the pipeline view. They reached leads late or not at all, they lost a lot of business, and management had no clue. So we asked, why even have a pipeline? An AI voice could pick up every lead right away, ask a few questions, and connect them to the first free salesperson. Then the stages fill themselves in, and management sees what's happening. We could have built that. But they had two months to their renewal, and their whole business ran through that CRM, so they couldn't even start.

Thirty days out, you decide. If you're staying, sign the new quote, for twelve months and no longer. If you're leaving, your replacement has to be ready by the end date. And if it won't be, I'd sign the twelve months anyway, and start now.
