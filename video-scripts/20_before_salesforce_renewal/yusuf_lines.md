## Step 1: Find your dates, and who gets the emails

Before anything else, find out when your contract ends, and who Salesforce is emailing about it. Go to your contracts page in Salesforce. The path's on screen. Write down the end date, and if it's on auto-renew.

I don't really see how thirty days and ninety days fit together, so I'd just go early and use a hundred days. If your contract ends on September thirtieth, put a reminder in your calendar for June twenty-second.

Salesforce sends its renewal emails to the billing contact on your contract. So if that person left last year, nobody at your company sees them. Check who it is, and change it today if it's wrong.

## Step 2: Read your order form

Next, read your order form. That's the document you actually signed, with your prices on it. If it disagrees with Salesforce's main contract, the order form wins. So your real deadline is whatever the order form says.

If you can't find it, there's an email on screen you can send your account rep.

First, check how long you signed for. The standard is twelve months, and I wouldn't go longer. One rep pitched a customer an eighty-five month term, and the customer wrote, my guy, I don't even know if software will exist then.

Then check when your discount ends, because after that you can be back at list price.

And check every product on it. If you don't use something, tell your rep in writing to take it out. One customer saved thirty thousand dollars by taking out a support plan, and their rep left it in the contract six times. So check the final version before you sign.

## Step 3: Count your licences

Then count your licences, because you can add them any day, but you can only remove them at the renewal. And they pile up, because nobody actually removes them.

Say you pay for twenty-five licences at two hundred dollars a month, and only eighteen people log in. Those seven unused licences cost you about seventeen thousand dollars a year.

But if you renew with fewer licences, Salesforce can set a new price per licence, so you can lose your discount. That customer from January says they halved their licences, and everything went to list price.

So ask Salesforce for a price on both, twenty-five licences and eighteen, and take the lower yearly total.

To get your own number, go to your licence page in Salesforce. The path's on screen. Then ask each manager who on their team really needs a licence next year.

## Step 4: Turn off auto-renew in writing, and get a price from another CRM

A hundred days before the end, turn off auto-renew in writing. I know that can feel like picking a fight. But it doesn't cancel Salesforce. It just means you'll have to sign a new quote before the end date, or Salesforce shuts off.

When you're selling, a contract that renews by itself is the easiest deal you'll ever get. So when you turn that off, Salesforce has to give you a reason to sign, and that's what gets you a better price.

In July, one company that had shrunk to twenty-something people did this, even though their sales rep kept telling them they didn't need to. They say that's what gave them much more power to negotiate.

To do it, send one email. It's on screen and in the description. It gives written notice under section eleven point two of the main contract, and tells them not to auto-renew. Then it asks for two quotes, one for the licences you have today and one for the number you need.

Also post a paper copy to the address in the contract, because another section says email may not count for a notice like this.

Then check that it worked, because starting early isn't enough. That customer from January emailed several times to turn off auto-renew, but their account page still said auto-renew. Then they asked for a cheaper licence, and nobody answered for a week. By then they were under thirty days, so Salesforce said it was too late.

They think going to Salesforce's legal and ethics team is what finally solved it. So if your account page still says auto-renew a week after your email, ask your rep to confirm in writing that it's off. And if they won't, that's where I'd go.

The same week, get a real price from another CRM, even if you don't plan to move. If Salesforce's price is the only one you have, you've got nothing to push back with. That company from July got an offer from HubSpot, gave it to Salesforce, and said they were running both CRMs to see which one won. They say it was ridiculous how much the price came down.

## Step 5: Ask if Salesforce fits

The last one is the one I care about most. Ask if Salesforce actually fits how you work, because a lower price doesn't fix that. That company from July got their lower price, and they say they might still leave next year, because it's a big tool for a lean team.

When you buy a CRM, what you're actually buying is a standard process that you have to adapt to. And nobody's really happy with someone else's process. Out of every hundred companies I worked with, maybe three were happy with the standard setup. If you want Salesforce to work your way, you have to pay developers. In my experience, people underestimate that work by something like five times.

To find out if Salesforce fits you, take one real customer from last month, and sit down with the people who did the work. Write down every step, from the first contact to getting paid. Then mark every step that happened outside Salesforce, like in a spreadsheet or in someone's head.

You might think, one spreadsheet's no big deal. After a while, it's just how things are. But that's work you're paying Salesforce for and still doing by hand.

If it's just one or two steps, Salesforce fits well enough, so take your lower price and renew. If it's more than that, a lower price won't fix it. And if the way you sell is what sets you apart, that's when I'd want my own system.

Stay or leave, decide at least thirty days before your contract ends. And if your new CRM won't be ready by the end date, I'd renew for twelve months and start on it now.
