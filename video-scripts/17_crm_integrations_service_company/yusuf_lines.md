## Proof

Say a customer calls you and asks, "Why did I get a reminder? I paid two weeks ago." You look it up. QuickBooks says paid, your CRM says they still owe you, and the job app says the job's still open.

So here are the five CRM integrations a service company needs, with one check for each. At the end I'll show you a plumbing company that runs most of this in one system.

## Who it's for

This is for you if your invoices live in QuickBooks and your jobs are in another app, and someone ends up typing the same customer into all of them.

## Draw your map

By the end, you'll have your whole setup on one page. Draw a box in the middle for your CRM, and a box for each tool around it. Each box gets two arrows, one going out and one coming back. If an arrow runs on its own, make it green. If a person does it by hand, make it red. And if nobody owns it, make it dotted.

## Integration 1: Accounting (QuickBooks)

Number one is accounting, and for a lot of service companies that's QuickBooks. Integrated means a won job puts the invoice in QuickBooks with the same lines the customer agreed to, and when the customer pays, your CRM knows.

In 2017 I told a customer that linking the CRM to accounting doesn't actually save any time, because finance still has to check the sales contacts by hand. I'd still say the typing is minor, maybe five percent.

What you get is the whole customer. Your team sees what someone bought, what they've paid, and when it's time to renew, so a salesperson knows who to call. And you can see which marketing actually brought in the money.

In my experience, about fifteen percent of companies had trouble getting the two to agree. In the CRM, you close the deal, and it's won. But accounting has a bunch of stages. You sent the invoice, you got paid, you didn't get paid the full amount. These systems have completely different ways of seeing the world.

Take HubSpot's QuickBooks connector. I checked these help pages in November 2026, and they change, so look at yours. The recommended setup makes the invoice sync one way, from QuickBooks into HubSpot. And if one payment covers three invoices, that payment doesn't reach HubSpot. So your CRM can still show those invoices as unpaid, and that's one way you get that phone call.

So someone checks QuickBooks every morning and marks deals paid by hand. Or nobody does, and a salesperson pitches a new job to a customer who still owes you for the last one.

Here's a check you can do today. Take your last five invoices. Did anyone re-type the lines from the quote? Can you see in the CRM that each one's paid, without opening QuickBooks? Every no is a red arrow on your map.

Then ask whoever set up the connector, "Which way does our QuickBooks sync run? And what happens when one payment covers several invoices?"

You might only need one arrow. Stijn Hurkmans, the CFO at Datenna, wrote, "I like HubSpot. I like our accounting set-up. I was only missing the bridge between them." So he built it. A won deal in HubSpot makes the invoice, finance approves it, and payments get checked against the books.

## Integration 2: Quotes and e-signature

Secondly, quotes and e-signature. When this works, the lines the customer signed become the job and the invoice, and the deal moves to won by itself.

The broken integration I remember best was at a company that sent a lot of quotes every day, to restaurants and other places. Their CRM couldn't handle their quoting rules, so they built their own quoting software and connected the two. You filled in a pile of fields, clicked a button, and a field came back saying approved or not approved. When that link failed, they just couldn't send quotes. And even when it worked, it was super messy. My old company took them on much later, and built a CRM around what they needed.

Put your last signed quote next to its CRM deal and its invoice. Is it the same total and the same lines? And did anybody have to type something to make them match?

## Integration 3: Jobs and scheduling

Number three is jobs and scheduling. You want a won deal to become a job without anyone copying it, and any extra hour or part to go back onto the invoice.

When I checked, Jobber's help page said its QuickBooks sync had become one way, from Jobber to QuickBooks, and paid status no longer syncs back. Your job app and your CRM both think they own the customer, and QuickBooks has its own copy.

One business manager at a construction company wrote about her job app, "Nobody has resolved our issue in our almost-two-years with SF of syncing." SF is Service Fusion.

Extras are where I'd look for money. If a crew does an extra hour and it stays on paper, you did that hour for free. Say you do three hundred jobs a year, and one in ten has two hundred dollars nobody billed. That's thirty jobs, so six thousand dollars a year. I don't know your number, but you can find it. Take your last twenty jobs, and put what the crew wrote down next to what went on the invoice.

Then look up one customer in all three systems. If the address doesn't match, pick one system as the home for customer details, and have the others copy from it.

## Integration 4: Email and calendar

Number four is email and calendar. Every customer email and meeting should land on that customer's record, where the whole team can see it.

Most people assume this one just works. But HubSpot's calendar page says it only syncs your primary calendar, and if the person you're meeting isn't a contact yet, HubSpot won't create them.

So people CC the CRM when they remember, and the shared inbox never gets logged. Then a salesperson leaves, and your best customer's history goes with them.

Sit down with your top seller and just say, "Can you open your last three customer meetings in the CRM with me? If they're missing, we connect your calendar today."

## Integration 5: The glue

And number five is the glue. That's everything between your tools with no real connector, like Zapier, or a person copying between screens. Nobody buys it on purpose. It just grows.

Byron Van Gisborne ran a recruitment business on six tools. He called it "Zapier and duct tape holding the whole monster together," and he wrote that his team spent more time copying information between systems than actually placing people.

Glue also breaks quietly. Zapier's help page says an automation turns itself off if ninety-five percent of its runs fail over seven days. So a tool renames a field, the automation fails, and it switches off. At my old company, we often found out from the customer.

So list every automation between your tools and every copy-paste job. You won't see most of the copying yourself, so ask your team. "Tell me anything you copy from one screen to another every week." Next to each one, write who hears when it breaks. No name means a dotted arrow.

## Is fixing it enough?

Some of this you can fix this week. But with a standard connector, someone else decided which way the data goes and which fields go. If you need an arrow it doesn't have, no setting will add it.

That plumbing company got around most of this. The owner built his own CRM, and it does his estimates, dispatching, scheduling, invoicing and payments. It's one system, so those arrows don't exist.

Here's one from our research with real numbers. One company, Springland, sells pet products wholesale. Gabriel Ficht built their CRM in about twenty hours of part-time work over two weeks. It pulls every inbox into one view per customer, along with the orders from every platform they sell on. He says sales ops time is down more than eighty percent, and they're staying on top of three to four times more deals. That's one company and his own numbers. But that's the kind of lift I'd look for when a team can see the whole customer.
