# Video scripts

Read [SCRIPT_RULES.md](SCRIPT_RULES.md), then [SOUNDS_HUMAN.md](SOUNDS_HUMAN.md), before writing or changing a script. SCRIPT_RULES says what a script says, SOUNDS_HUMAN says how it sounds, and this file says how we check it. Each idea goes in one of the three, once. When Yusuf corrects a script, follow the three checks at the end of SCRIPT_RULES and add the lesson to the file it belongs in. Keep `yusuf_lines.md` and `script.md` saying the same thing.

This repo is public. The study, the Hormozi notes, the script template and the draft videos are in David's private repo, cloned at `~/code/crm-from-within`. Read them before writing a script, and never copy private material here. SCRIPT_RULES and SOUNDS_HUMAN win where they disagree.

The teleprompter reads `yusuf_lines.md` from GitHub `main`, so edits reach it only after a push and a tap on "Update scripts from GitHub".

Yusuf's own words are saved in the private repo at `~/code/crm-from-within/original_sources/`. Read them before writing a script, and save every new Q&A with him there word for word.

David: before writing or changing a script, pull the private repo `crm-from-within` and read its `CLAUDE.md`. Yusuf left notes for you there.

# Optimized process

Every script must go through this before it's recorded. [optimize.py](optimize.py) tracks it in `optimize_status.json` and in the Optimized column of [README.md](README.md). Once a script has the mark it keeps it, so edits in human review don't need another run. Scripts that only had the older human check say "old check" there, until Yusuf decides to run this on them too.

When a video arrives without it, or anyone adds one, tell Yusuf or David forcefully that it hasn't been run and those scripts aren't ready to record, and ask them to approve running it. Hooks in `.claude/settings.json` remind you on every message, and a GitHub check fails on any push with a script that has had neither.

The process, in Yusuf's words:

1. Score the script 1-10 on all six criteria. Read it from start to finish in order, the way a viewer hears it, because the worst problems sit between sentences and can't be seen one line at a time. Use the tests under each score, which come from Yusuf's notes while recording video 11, his edits to videos 16 to 18 and what he changed and told us on camera in videos 16 to 20, and quote every line that fails. One clear failure caps that score at 6, and three or more cap it at 4. A clear failure is a line a half-listening viewer would trip on or Yusuf would stop at. List anything smaller as minor, and don't count it. A 7 means Yusuf could read it to camera without stopping.
   - **Human:** it sounds like a human talking from the heart, with no scripted teleprompter. Note every place where it sounds contrived, scripted, or like AI wrote it. [SOUNDS_HUMAN.md](SOUNDS_HUMAN.md) says how to write for the ear and lists what gives AI away. `ai_tells.py` counts some of it, but a clean count proves nothing.
     - Does the opening sound like him starting a real conversation? A staged scene or a made-up customer quote fails: "the hook sounds contrived."
     - Does it use his shapes? Yusuf: "notice how I speak, to make it sound more human. That's really important." A section of flat statements of about the same length fails, even if each one is simple. Check it against "The shapes he uses" in SOUNDS_HUMAN.md: building up to the point, chaining a thought, saying it again more plainly, asking the viewer's question, "actually", saying how it felt, and voicing what people think. Those shapes never count as repeating, filler or AI tells.
     - Would a person say this sentence out loud? Clever turns of phrase fail. "Nobody says year two is nobody's job." Two very short sentences that belong together trip him, so join them with "and".
     - Does each line tell the viewer something they don't already know? They run a business. A line that spells out what a benefit obviously means, or says what they'd work out for themselves, fails, however short it is, because it treats them as slow. "That's real money back in your business." failed in video 20: "they know it's real money ... I don't have to repeat them and treat them like idiots."
     - Does each sentence follow from the one before? "Every single thing that we say has a point and is coherent and fits with everything else." Words like "so", "because" and "but" carry the reasoning, so keep them.
     - Does it sound like someone advising the boss, or like a manual? A step that only says what to do fails where the viewer would ask "why should I?"
     - Does he say what he really thinks? Where the viewer is probably wrong, say why they think it, then say so. A section that stays neutral where he'd have an opinion fails.
     - Does he give the honest size of things? A picture of someone failing, a small problem painted as a disaster or a sure prediction fails. He says what's realistic, gives a range, or leaves the call to the person who'd know. In video 19 he dropped "walks into that call blind" for "it can be really important", and "one real day" became "a few days or maybe a few weeks".
     - Does he say what the viewer feels about the job? Where a step sounds dull or hard, he says so, then says if it's easier than it sounds: "this is kind of the boring part, I know", "it's actually not as difficult as it might sound". Once or twice in a video is enough. Say it literally. A figure of speech for the feeling fails: say what would really happen, then why that's fine. "I know that can feel like picking a fight" stopped him, and became "Salesforce might push you a bit on this".
   - **Easy to understand:** how easy, simple and straightforward is the script to understand? Do people need to focus, concentrate or think twice, or does it flow so they just immediately understand, in simple, direct language, what we're talking about? This is a separate score from Human: a line can sound natural and still lose people. The "Write for the ear" rules in SOUNDS_HUMAN.md apply here too.
     - After the first two sentences, could the viewer say what this video covers and why it's worth watching? A story, a scene, a question or a conclusion that comes before the topic fails: "I don't think it's clear what this video is about." He wants "something that makes them understand immediately the problem this video addresses".
     - Is every phrase in the opening clear with nothing heard yet? A phrase that only makes sense once the video has explained it fails, however catchy it is. Say the result they'd get, in plain words. "Without one big, risky move" failed in the first lines of video 20: "how would they know what I mean". He said "so that you can leave Salesforce before the next one."
     - Is each paragraph about one thing? Two good ideas joined because both are true fail. Finish one, and put the other where it belongs. "There's no bill per person. With AI, it can update itself." failed: "those are two unrelated topics, you shouldn't mix them."
     - Is shorthand unpacked? A step title's label, a word like "that" after a new section, or a phrase that's only clear on the page fails. Say what it means right after, in full. "Find your dates" got "what I mean by that is the date when your renewal is actually going to happen". "One with each number" became "one without reducing licenses, and one with fewer licenses". "That sounds like a big step" became "building your own CRM with a renewal coming up".
     - Could someone half listening get every sentence the first time? If they'd have to stop and work it out, it fails.
     - Does the viewer already know what each word points at? "If you walk in without your own list, you end up using the seller's." fails, because nobody has mentioned a list: "What list are you talking about?"
     - Would someone who started listening at this sentence know what it's about? Don't count on a label set up minutes earlier. "The first moment is when you want your CRM to work a little differently." fails: "The first moment of what?"
     - Is every step between two ideas said out loud? Leaving a step for the viewer to work out fails.
     - Does every claim and instruction finish its thought? Say what happens otherwise, or why that's enough, in its own clause. A reason squeezed into a few words or left out fails, however short it is. Where one thing leads to another, say why it does. "The more time you have, the better price you can get" isn't finished until "because you can prepare for that renewal", so you "have leverage". He says it in full on camera: "because otherwise it would just renew for a whole new year", "because if those work then the rest will probably work too".
     - If the video covers two ways of doing something, does it say so in plain words before the first step? An aside like "as we go, I'll mention" fails, and he stopped at that line in video 19: "I'm going to cover both methods here."
     - Is a callback easy to say? Point at where it came from first, then say the thing. A bare name from minutes earlier fails: "Remember, Paul's weekend" took two tries and became "Remember the example I gave. Paul's weekend".
     - Is the conclusion said in plain words? Name the thing instead of "that" or "yours".
     - Before an example starts, does the viewer hear that it's real and where it comes from? A story that starts with a name or a company and no such line fails, even if it names the topic.
     - Can the viewer picture each kind of tool? Naming only the kind of tool fails. The first time it comes up, give two popular tools a small company would use: "name 2 examples of the most popular SMB tools for that thing."
   - **Interest:** captures and holds our buyer's interest: a sales manager or a CEO of a company of at least ten people paying real money for its CRM.
     - Why would this viewer listen? Write one plain sentence: what do they get from this video that they don't have now? Then quote the line in the first paragraph that says it. A topic or a list fails, however clear: "I'll show you the five a service company needs" doesn't say what that gets them. Name the gain itself, once, and don't comment on how good it is. Lead with what they'd gain for their business, because that beats only finding out what's broken: "the right ones can lift your whole business. You'd see who's worth calling and which ads really bring in money." Yusuf: "what's the value they get from this vid?" A failure here caps Interest at 5.
     - Does it tell the viewer something has changed? Say what they've had to live with, and why that's no longer true: "in the past you had no choice ... but today, that's not the case at all." Useful tips that leave them where they started fail.
     - Is what's changed a new way of doing this job? "You could have a CRM you own" fails, because that's a product. Name the new way, say what they no longer have to do, how it works instead and where they end up, on this video's own date or event if it has one: "by the day that your next renewal comes up next year you will have a full-fledged CRM running in parallel". Do it where it first comes up, not only near the end. Video 19: "a completely new way to migrate", "you no longer actually have to do this huge one-time migration", "suddenly one day you'll notice that oh we have all our data already".
     - Does a story become a risk the viewer shares? Say what it shows about every company in that position, in one line: "you don't actually know until you start using it regardless of what the salesperson says". A story that stays someone else's bad luck fails.
     - Does an example go all the way to what it costs or gets the business? One that stops at the symptom, or at a question, fails. "Do quotes take days to go out?" went on to "a small delay like that can cause you to get a huge delay in revenue or even lose the momentum and lose the deal".
     - Where the viewer could stop at the practical fix, is the reason to go further the real one? Say what renting keeps doing to them, which is making them adapt their business to someone else's system. Having to repeat a chore isn't that reason. "You'd still be doing these same steps next year" became "you'd still be renting someone else's system".
     - Does every section move the video's question forward? "The whole next section completely irrelevant." If it doesn't help the viewer decide or act, it goes, however true it is.
     - Is every example recent? "What the hell are you bringing up examples from 2016." A year that makes a story sound old fails.
     - Does the video deliver everything it promises at the start? Promise only what the core point needs. In video 17 the opening promised checks and two examples, and he skipped both.
     - Does it sell what's possible, or hunt for what's broken? Say what the viewer could have before what might be wrong. A script framed as a repair fails, and so does proof that only shows a limit in someone else's product. In video 17 he dropped three help pages and his own broken-connection story. Where the normal option is good, say so, then say owning is better: "integrating a quoting tool is highly recommended", then "some companies think it's even better if you don't have two separate tools."
     - Does it say why the problem happens, from what each person wants? Saying only what goes wrong fails.
     - Does it say what really happens, step by step? Naming a risk fails. "If that went wrong, your salespeople couldn't sell" became "the old system, you had to cancel it so that it won't renew, and you had to migrate everything before the cancellation period, I mean, it's a huge headache."
     - Does the opening story carry through? It comes back in the teaching and shows the core point working: "this doesn't require them to replace anything at all. But what it does do is it starts getting their data into the new system." A story dropped after the opening fails. If the source doesn't say what happened next, ask Yusuf and never invent it.
     - Does it answer the viewer's "that's no big deal"?
   - **Usable value:** does the viewer leave knowing what's possible for their business and what to do next, without us? Entertainment viewers don't turn into education viewers, so go all in on teaching.
     - Does each step say what it gets their business, past the obvious? Less typing is the obvious one, and he calls it minor. "These tools often tell you when they open the quote", so "you can do outreach at the right moment", passes. A step that only says what the tool should do fails.
     - Does each main step teach what someone who's done this job for many companies knows? A list of things to write down or look at fails. Say which part matters, which part to ignore and why, and the quick way to get what you need. That judgment is the value. In video 19 "write down your automations" became which ones are background updates to ignore, why they don't matter, and getting the people who know in a room instead. Bring it from the sources, and if it's new, list it for him to confirm.
     - Does it say who does this? Name who in the company does it or decides, and why that's enough. "Check that it's right" with nobody doing the checking fails. He said "have a salesperson check if everything important is there".
     - Are the steps different lengths? Put most of the teaching in the one or two steps where the real know-how is, and keep the rest to a few lines. Steps of about equal length fail. In video 19 he spent five minutes on steps 1 and 2, and two and a half on steps 3 to 5.
     - Would he actually do this check? A step that makes the viewer audit something ("take your last twenty jobs, and compare") fails, unless that check is what the video promises. He stopped at the first of five in video 17, "No, skip that.", and dropped them in videos 5 and 16 too. Say what they'd gain instead.
     - Does each step come out of what was just said, so the viewer knows why they're doing it? A task dropped in after a story fails.
     - Does it say how to decide? "If it's just one or two systems, you can probably do it" beats "count the systems".
     - Does every step serve the core point? Yusuf skipped the counting rules, the sixty-day check, who fixes it and when to shut off the old CRM, and he kept one thing to do. A useful step that doesn't serve the core point fails.
     - Does it show what to look for, or make the viewer do sums? He skipped the lost-deals sum and asked instead: "did you not follow up, did you not do the best possible call, did salespeople need more coaching?" A sum said aloud fails.
     - With a lot to do, does it say what comes first? "The biggest opportunity for the least cost and least risk first" beats a list.
     - Where a step works around a rented CRM or separate tools, does the viewer also hear the other option right there, which is having that built into a CRM they own? Keep the step and give both, so they can choose: "you can do all of those integrations, or you can build it into the crm itself". Saving it all for one section at the end fails. Do this only where owning is a real advantage on this step's topic. A step with no real advantage leaves owning out, because a slot filled with a contrast is worse than nothing. He dropped "there's simply no end date" in video 20: "Okay, skip that."
   - **Belief:** makes that CEO or sales manager believe that a custom-built CRM can lift their business to new heights. Not just saving some money, but actually making their business able to solve some bottleneck or do something valuable that has material impact.
     - Does the core point lead to owning one system? Our USP is one system that runs a company's entire process, from finding customers to delivering the work, fully adapted to them, with no integrations, and they own it. Owning has to be the video's thread, reached from its own topic. In the first third he tells the viewer directly, saying "you", that they could have this in a system built around them that they own. Another company's story or a hint doesn't count. Then that option comes back at the steps where it's a real advantage on that step's topic, usually two or three, so they leave seeing the choice between patching a rented CRM and owning one. A video with numbered steps can pass, because the practical steps stay. A video that only turns to owning near the end fails, however useful it is. That failure caps Belief at 4, and no line edit can fix it.
     - Does owning add something a connection can't? "The same thing, without syncing" fails if it's all the video says. Say what only a system of your own can do, from this step's topic, as what the viewer gets: "a system that updates itself, so salespeople can spend their time on customers". What AI makes possible should be big, like a CRM that does the admin itself, but it goes where it fits the topic, and the picture of one system is the natural place. Say something new each time. A video where AI never does real work in front of the viewer fails.
     - Is each owning point followed through? One line of contrast fails, like saying a rented thing has a limit and an owned one doesn't. Stay on that one advantage and say in two or three sentences what it unlocks for the business: "there is no bill per person, you literally can add every single person in your entire organization", so all your teams are in the same system and "you can actually get a lot more out of the system that way". A stock line about AI added after it fails, because it's a second topic.
     - Does it walk through how owning handles this step, at least once? A claim about it fails. Take one example and say in order what gets built, what happens to the work or the data, and where that leaves them, so the viewer could explain it. Add the safety net that makes it believable. In video 19 "the data moves over piece by piece" became building a quoting tool that picks up the information over time, so "by the time that you actually do shut off your old system the most important information will already be migrated", and "keep like one license of the old system alive".
     - Does it say why owning can be better and cheaper? "You're paying for 97 features that you're never going to use" passes. "It's expensive to connect tools" alone doesn't.
     - Does it take away what stops them owning a CRM? Name what makes it feel too risky or too big, from this video's topic, and show how that goes away, like building it "step by step while you keep working in the old system".
     - Can the viewer picture it? Before the pitch, walk through their own work running in that one system, saying "you", using what this video taught, so they think "how good and efficient that would be". Start from what renting makes them put up with. Then follow one lead through, and at each point say what the system does by itself and what the person still does. End on what they no longer have to put up with, and on it being built around how they work. A list of steps where each just happens in one place isn't a picture: he failed twice at reading one in video 20, then said "when you rent someone else's system, it comes with a lot of bloat", "it reads your emails, it listens to your calls", "the system designs a quote around what it heard", the salespeople "just go in and approve the quote or edit some things", and "you don't have to change your company to fit someone else's system". Don't end on "you'd never do this again" or on salespeople only talking to customers, because he rejected both. Neither is another company's story, or a pitch that only lists what we do. It usually takes about a minute of spoken time, so don't squeeze it.
     - Right after his credentials, does he say what he does now? Credentials that stop at his old company fail, even if the pitch names his company later: "Now, I run a company that builds custom CRMs for you."
     - Does it answer the money doubt with how we work? "You'll pay for both for a while" left alone fails. He said: "we don't actually charge monthly, we simply charge for projects during this transition phase, so you don't actually have two recurring bills."
     - Can the viewer do this without buying? Say how, in a way that fits the video. Where the video says doing it alone is risky, "at least get advice from someone who has done it many times before" passes and "you can do all of this on your own" fails. A denial that he's selling isn't needed. A close that only sells fails.
     - Does the pitch match what this video taught? Reword any standard pitch line that fights the argument, and tie the pitch to the risk the video just took away. In video 19 he said "we move your data", then "no", then "we build it step by step so that you never risk this huge migration".
     - Does it tell them to keep their CRM? He stopped at "just keep it. but no." Invite them instead: "if you think this is interesting and you want to start by just researching what this could mean for you".
     - Does his old company look good? A line that suggests its product failed, or that it missed something, fails.
     - Does the proof stand on its own? "That's one comment, and I can't check it." and "We never got to test that." both fail: "that sounds incredibly like AI" and "what's the point of writing that?"
     - Does the story show a business getting better? A story about why someone couldn't start builds no belief, so carry it on to what's being done about it.
     - Does an outside case show this video's point? He cut a stranger's cheaper contract: "No, forget that."
     - Does the reasoning lead the viewer to owning a CRM? A pile of cases doesn't do that.
   - **Shorten:** is the script as short as it can be while making the same points? "Remove unnecessary paragraphs that don't add value. Reformulate sentences and paragraphs that can be refactored so that they're shorter and easier to understand while getting across the same value / point." (Yusuf, 5 Nov 2026)
     - Does each section have its own job? Name each in a few words. No job: cut it. A job or point said twice: merge them.
     - Can this sentence or paragraph say the same thing in fewer, simpler words? If a clearly shorter version works as well, it fails. Give that version. A shorter version that loses one of his shapes doesn't work as well.
     - Is the shorter version still the same point? A cut that loses a fact, a step in the reasoning or a joining word lowers this score too.
     - Would he read this or skip it? He talks about twice as long as the script on what he keeps, so a script over about 1,000 spoken words is too long. Fewer points, said simply, leave him room to explain. Keep the steps a title promises, most of them in a few lines.
     - Does the pitch repeat what the video already said? If the teaching showed how we work, the pitch needs a line or two. The fit check tied to the biggest idea and the link at the end are two different jobs, so don't fail them as a repeat.
     - A line that gives the reason, says what happens otherwise, answers the viewer's doubt, says what he really thinks, shows the option of owning, walks through how it works or paints the picture of it is the point, so don't fail it for length. The option of owning can come back after a step where it's a real advantage, as long as it says something new for that step.
2. Add it up: Human + Easy to understand + Interest + Usable value + Belief + Shorten, out of 60. Stop once the core point passes, Human and Easy to understand are both at least 6 and two rounds in a row haven't raised Human, Easy to understand and Shorten together, or after six rounds. Each scorer finds different faults, so one flat round can be chance.
3. Rewrite it to raise Human, Easy to understand and Shorten, without losing any informational value or making it less exciting to listen to. Those three drive the rewrite (Yusuf, 5 Nov 2026), once the core point is right.
   - Fix the core point first. If the scorer's core point check fails, line edits can't help, so this round is a new script. Write the new argument, keep every step that still serves it, add the option of owning and the picture of it, and cut what no longer fits. This is the one round that may add words. Yusuf had to do this himself for videos 16 and 17 on 6 Nov 2026, after the process had polished their old core points to 46 and 47 of 60. Never change a core point Yusuf set himself.
   - Fix the viewer's value next. If the scorer's VALUE line fails, write the sentence saying what this viewer gets, then rewrite the opening around it. If the script reads as a repair job, that's a new script too: for each step, write what it gets their business and what owning adds, cut the checks and the proof of what's wrong with other tools, and let the pitch and the picture of one system stand. Yusuf did this himself on camera in video 17. The first paragraph says the problem, then what they'd gain for their business. Check that the video delivers exactly that, and that its later sections serve it. Polishing an opening that gives no reason to listen doesn't help.
   - Human and Easy to understand come first: start with the lines that failed those two. If a change helps any other score but makes a line sound less human or harder to follow, don't make it, even if it's shorter.
   - The other three are checks. Fix a clear failure, but don't chase the number, because scores move by chance and fixing every note is how video 11 got patched into nonsense.
   - Start from the video's argument, and make every paragraph serve it. In the first rewrite, write each section again with the old script closed, the way he'd talk it through, because editing the old lines keeps their squeezed sentences. When a later round pays words back, take out a whole point from a minor step, never a reason or who does it. Write it the way he talks when he fixes a line: his rewrites are in `original_sources/2026-11-05_video_11_notes_to_claude.md` in the private repo.
   - Before you keep a line, ask if it's there only because a test asked for it. A test says what the viewer needs, not that a sentence must fill that slot. If the point is already clear, or the step has nothing real to say, leave it out.
   - Never add a case, caveat or detail just to lift a score, and cut as many words as each fix adds. In video 11 each round added proof and removed joining words, so the score went up while the script got worse.
   - A reason, an answer to the viewer's doubt, an honest opinion, the option of owning or the picture of it isn't a detail. Add them where a section reads like a manual or stops at a tip, and cut elsewhere to pay for the words.
4. Repeat from step 1. When it stops, use the round with the highest Human + Easy to understand as the main script, then the highest total, then the later one. Leave round 1 out of that choice, because scorers differ by a point or two and the script as it came in shouldn't win on that. Before calling it done, read the finished script from start to finish yourself, as the viewer would. The score doesn't replace that. Fixes after that change the words, so score the finished version as the last round. That's the score the mark shows.

Have a fresh agent score each round, one that hasn't seen the earlier scores, so nobody grades their own rewrite. Save every round in the private repo at `videos/optimized/`, then record it: `python3 video-scripts/optimize.py tick 10 "7,6,6,7,5,6 8,8,7,8,7,7 8,8,7,8,6,7"`, with one human,easy,interest,value,belief,shorten group per round. Scores from before 5 Nov 2026 used the old five with Human doubled and show as "old score", so don't compare them with today's totals. Marks made before Shorten counted Value per word in its place.

# Notes Yusuf says on camera

From video 11 on, Yusuf may say "For Claude: ..." in a recording and then carry on. When you process a recording, find those notes, save his words in `original_sources/`, apply them to every unrecorded script they fit and rerun the Optimized process on those scripts. Put each note on the editor's cut list. Recorded scripts stay as they are.

# Read and approved by a human

David or Yusuf reads every script before Yusuf records it, so obviously bad lines get fixed before filming. It's the last column of [README.md](README.md). Run the Optimized process first and read last. When David or Yusuf says they've read and approved a script, mark it: `python3 video-scripts/optimize.py read 09 David`. Then commit and push.

Mark a script only when that person says so. Never mark one yourself. Any edit to the spoken lines clears this mark, so tell them it needs reading again.
