## Point 1: What happened

In February 2026, software stocks fell hard. The fear was simple. If AI agents do the work, companies need fewer people. So vendors sell fewer seats. By mid-year, SaaStr wrote that HubSpot's stock was down about fifty-six percent that year. And Salesforce was valued far below the rest of software. The number is on screen.

Now, that's just investors making bets. It doesn't tell you anything about your CRM. But what they're scared of is the seat.

## Point 2: Why seats are the problem

I checked HubSpot's pricing page when I recorded this, in November 2026. Sales Hub Professional was ninety dollars a seat per month, billed yearly. So for twenty people, that's about twenty-two thousand dollars a year. And that's before onboarding and add-ons. Prices change, so take that as an example.

Here's the thing. A seat price only makes sense if the value grows with your headcount. If software does more of the work, your bill should shrink. But the seat model says it grows.

So try this. Divide your yearly CRM bill by the number of people who open it every day. That's your cost per real user.

## Point 3: What's hype

Let's start with the hype. Number one is "nobody will pay for software anymore." That's just wrong. Companies will keep paying for software that does a job. The real question is the price per head.

Number two, "AI builds your CRM in an afternoon." It can build a working screen in an afternoon, sure. But a CRM your business runs on for five years needs a lot more. Your data has to move properly. You need permissions and backups. And you need someone to fix it when it breaks.

Number three is "the vendors are dropping seats." But look at Salesforce's own AI pricing page. They charge two dollars per conversation. And there's still a price per user on top. HubSpot's plans now come with credits too. So the seat isn't gone. They're just stacking usage on top of it.

## Point 4: What's real

Now here's what's real. Building software got much cheaper and faster. What used to take a team a year, a small team can now build in weeks. AI writes a lot of the code, and people check it.

For years you had two choices. You could rent a standard CRM and bend to fit it. Or you could pay a fortune for a custom build. Now there's a third option. A CRM built around how you sell and deliver, at a price a twenty-person company can afford. And the code belongs to you.

And CRM is all we build, so we've seen most of the problems before. You don't pay per seat, so the bill doesn't grow every time you hire. And you don't have to build anything yourself. We build it, and we keep it running.

Someone who runs sales at a large software company told us he doesn't care about owning code. He cares about getting the same features or better, for less. And that's fair. Owning is only worth it if it also works better for you.

So ask your team straight out. "What do you do every week outside the CRM?" In a spreadsheet, a notebook, or in their head. Write down three things. A CRM built for you would cover those. A rented one doesn't.

## Point 5: Who should still rent

So who should keep renting? If you're under ten people, a cheap standard CRM is fine. If your sales really are simple, a rented CRM already fits. You find a lead, you call, you win. And if nobody in your company will look after it, renting is simpler. The vendor does that for you.

Owning makes sense when the bill grows with every hire, and the work that actually matters happens in spreadsheets around the CRM.
